South Florida faces a growing threat from flooding, driven by rising sea levels, intense rainfall, and a very porous landscape. Because much of the region sits on permeable limestone, water can rise up from below as easily as it falls from above. That makes traditional barriers less effective. Climate change is speeding up these risks, with projections suggesting that sea levels could rise by more than a foot within the next few decades. As a result, communities from Miami Beach to the Keys are seeing more frequent "sunny day" flooding during high tides. Dealing with this will require a approach that includes upgraded drainage systems, elevated infrastructure, and long-term planning. Ultimately, protecting South Florida's future depends on decisive action from policymakers, residents, and scientists working together.

The United States spends more on health care per person than any other country, yet it ranks below many peer countries on things like life expectancy and access to care. One big reason for this gap is how complicated the system is. It relies on a patchwork of private insurers, employer plans, and government programs. Administrative costs alone take up a large share of total spending, pulling money away from patient care. Millions of Americans are still uninsured or underinsured, so many delay treatment until their conditions get more serious and more expensive. Countries with universal health care, like Canada and the UK, show that it's possible to cover everyone at a lower overall cost. By cutting administration and expanding coverage, the US could improve health outcomes and take pressure off families.
